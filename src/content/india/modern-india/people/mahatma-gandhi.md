Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi was one of the most influential political leaders of the Indian independence movement.

Born in 1869 in Porbandar, Gandhi trained as a lawyer and spent important years in South Africa, where he developed methods of political resistance that later became central to his work in India.

After returning to India, he became increasingly involved in the struggle against British colonial rule.

His political career connected mass protest, civil disobedience, social reform, and debates about the future of Indian society.

## Early Life

Gandhi was born on 2 October 1869 in Porbandar in present-day Gujarat.

He came from a Hindu family with a background in regional administration.

Gandhi travelled to London to study law and was called to the bar in 1891.

After returning to India, he initially struggled to establish a successful legal career.

His later political development was shaped much more strongly by his experiences in South Africa.

## South Africa

Gandhi travelled to South Africa in 1893 to work as a lawyer.

There he encountered racial discrimination directed against the Indian community.

His experiences contributed to a transformation in his political thinking.

Gandhi became involved in organizing Indian communities and opposing discriminatory laws.

During these struggles, he developed the concept of satyagraha.

The term is generally associated with a form of resistance based on truth, moral discipline, and nonviolent action.

South Africa therefore became an important laboratory for political methods that Gandhi later adapted to Indian conditions.

## Return to India

Gandhi returned to India permanently in 1915.

He travelled widely through different parts of the country and studied local political and social conditions.

His early campaigns in India included struggles involving peasants and agricultural communities.

These movements helped establish his reputation as a political leader who could connect national politics with local grievances.

## Champaran

In 1917, Gandhi became involved in the Champaran movement in Bihar.

Indigo cultivators faced difficult conditions under arrangements that required them to cultivate indigo for European planters.

Gandhi investigated the grievances of cultivators and organized a campaign around their demands.

The Champaran movement became one of his earliest major political interventions in India.

It also demonstrated how local economic grievances could be connected to a wider politics of organized resistance.

## Satyagraha

Satyagraha became one of the defining concepts of Gandhi's political practice.

It differed from ordinary political confrontation because it emphasized disciplined resistance rather than physical violence.

Participants were expected to accept personal suffering rather than respond to repression with violence.

For Gandhi, nonviolence was both a political method and a moral principle.

The approach was adapted differently in different campaigns, and its practical implementation often depended on local circumstances.

## Non-Cooperation Movement

After the First World War, political tensions in India intensified.

The Rowlatt Acts of 1919 extended extraordinary powers to the colonial government, generating widespread opposition.

The Jallianwala Bagh massacre in Amritsar in April 1919 further intensified anger toward British rule.

Gandhi subsequently became a central figure in the Non-Cooperation Movement launched in 1920.

The movement encouraged Indians to withdraw cooperation from colonial institutions.

People were encouraged to boycott government schools, courts, official honors, and imported goods.

The campaign brought large numbers of people into nationalist politics.

## Withdrawal of the Movement

The Non-Cooperation Movement was suspended in 1922 after violence at Chauri Chaura in Uttar Pradesh.

A group of protesters attacked and killed police personnel after a confrontation.

Gandhi regarded the violence as incompatible with the principles of the movement.

His decision to suspend the campaign demonstrated the importance he placed on nonviolence, even when doing so weakened immediate political momentum.

## The Salt March

One of Gandhi's most famous campaigns began in 1930.

The British colonial government maintained a monopoly over the production and taxation of salt.

Gandhi chose the salt laws as a focus for civil disobedience because salt was a basic necessity used by people across social and economic divisions.

He and his followers marched from Sabarmati Ashram to the coastal village of Dandi.

At the coast, Gandhi broke the salt law by making salt.

The action became the symbolic beginning of a much wider Civil Disobedience Movement.

## Mass Participation

The independence movement expanded beyond elite political organizations during Gandhi's period of leadership.

Students, workers, peasants, merchants, women, and other groups participated in different campaigns.

The scale of participation varied across regions and communities.

Not everyone supported Gandhi or agreed with his methods.

Nevertheless, nationalist politics increasingly became a mass phenomenon rather than a movement restricted primarily to educated political elites.

## Gandhi and the Indian National Congress

Gandhi became a central figure within the Indian National Congress.

He helped transform the organization into a broader political movement capable of mobilizing large sections of the population.

At the same time, the Congress contained many competing political ideas.

Leaders differed over constitutional reform, economic policy, socialism, communal representation, industrialization, and the pace and methods of independence.

Gandhi therefore operated within a political movement that was never completely unified around a single ideology.

## Social Reform

Gandhi's political activity extended beyond the demand for independence.

He campaigned against untouchability and supported greater social participation for communities that had experienced severe caste discrimination.

He also promoted village industries, hand-spinning, and the use of khadi.

For Gandhi, economic self-reliance was connected to political independence.

His emphasis on village society and small-scale production also generated disagreements with other Indian nationalists who favored rapid industrialization.

## The Quit India Movement

During the Second World War, the political conflict between Indian nationalists and the British government intensified.

In 1942, the Indian National Congress launched the Quit India Movement.

Gandhi called for an end to British rule and demanded immediate independence.

The colonial government responded by arresting Gandhi and other major Congress leaders.

Large protests and acts of resistance followed in several parts of India.

The movement was suppressed, but the political legitimacy of continued British rule had become increasingly difficult to sustain.

## Independence and Partition

India became independent on 15 August 1947.

Independence was accompanied by the partition of British India into India and Pakistan.

Partition resulted in enormous communal violence and one of the largest mass migrations of the twentieth century.

Gandhi opposed communal hatred and spent the final period of his life travelling through areas affected by violence and attempting to encourage reconciliation.

He did not occupy a formal government position in independent India.

His influence during this period came primarily through moral and political authority.

## Death

Gandhi was assassinated in New Delhi on 30 January 1948 by Nathuram Godse.

His death occurred only months after Indian independence.

The assassination shocked India and produced widespread mourning.

Gandhi's death also marked the end of one of the most influential political careers of the independence movement.

## Historical Legacy

Gandhi remains one of the most debated figures in modern Indian history.

His methods of nonviolent resistance influenced political movements beyond India.

His ideas about self-rule, political participation, economic life, religion, social reform, and nonviolence have also generated substantial debate.

Supporters have emphasized his role in mass mobilization and anti-colonial resistance.

Critics and historians have questioned aspects of his social and political thought, including his positions on caste, industrialization, and relations with other political movements.

His historical importance therefore lies not only in his personal beliefs but also in the political transformation associated with the movements in which he participated.

Gandhi's career illustrates the transition of Indian nationalism from a largely elite political movement into a mass struggle for independence, while also revealing the profound disagreements that shaped India's path toward freedom.
