Akbar was the third Mughal emperor and one of the most consequential rulers of early modern South Asia.

He inherited the Mughal throne as a young ruler in 1556, when Mughal authority remained vulnerable after the temporary loss of imperial control under his father, Humayun.

Over the course of his reign, Akbar expanded Mughal power across much of northern and central India while developing institutions that allowed the empire to govern a diverse population and an increasingly large territory.

## Coming to Power

Akbar became emperor in 1556 after the death of his father, Humayun.

Because Akbar was still young, the early years of his reign were heavily influenced by his regent, Bairam Khan.

The Mughal position was threatened by the Afghan ruler Hemu, who had captured Delhi and Agra.

The Mughal victory at the Second Battle of Panipat in 1556 restored imperial control over Delhi and helped secure Akbar's position.

As Akbar grew older, he gradually assumed personal authority over the government.

## Expansion of the Mughal Empire

Akbar's reign was marked by sustained territorial expansion.

Mughal forces consolidated control over much of northern India and extended imperial authority toward Gujarat, Rajasthan, Bengal, and central India.

The conquest of Gujarat gave the empire access to important commercial routes and ports.

Campaigns in Rajasthan brought several Rajput states into relationships with the Mughal Empire.

Not every Rajput polity accepted Mughal authority. Some resisted militarily, most famously at the siege of Chittor.

Akbar combined military pressure with diplomacy, marriage alliances, political appointments, and the incorporation of regional elites into imperial service.

This combination became an important feature of Mughal expansion.

## The Mughal Administrative System

Akbar's empire required an administrative structure capable of managing territory, taxation, military service, and political relationships.

The mansabdari system developed during his reign and provided a framework for ranking imperial officials and assigning them military and administrative responsibilities.

Mansabdars received numerical ranks that reflected their position within the imperial hierarchy.

Many officials were compensated through assignments of revenue from particular territories rather than simply through fixed salaries.

This helped connect military service, political status, and imperial administration.

## Revenue Administration

Agricultural taxation was central to the Mughal state.

Akbar's administration worked toward more systematic methods of assessing agricultural production and state revenue.

The reforms associated with Raja Todar Mal are particularly important in the development of Mughal revenue administration.

Land was surveyed and revenue assessments were increasingly based on recorded information about cultivation and agricultural yields.

The resulting system varied across regions and changed over time, but it contributed to the empire's ability to extract and manage agricultural revenue on a large scale.

## Relations with Rajput Rulers

Akbar's relationship with Rajput rulers was an important part of Mughal political strategy.

Some Rajput rulers entered alliances with the Mughal court and received important positions within the imperial administration and military.

Rajput nobles could therefore become participants in the Mughal political order rather than remaining permanently outside it.

The policy was not based simply on religious identity.

Political loyalty, military service, dynastic relationships, and regional interests all influenced the relationship between the Mughal court and Rajput states.

The resulting alliances strengthened the empire while also creating new connections between different political elites.

## Religion and Intellectual Exchange

Akbar became known for encouraging debate among representatives of different religious and intellectual traditions.

At his court, scholars and religious figures associated with Islam, Hindu traditions, Jainism, Christianity, and other intellectual traditions participated in discussions.

The Ibadat Khana at Fatehpur Sikri became associated with these debates.

Akbar's interest was not limited to establishing a single theological position.

His policies reflected an effort to develop an imperial order capable of governing a population with diverse religious traditions.

His approach to religious policy changed over the course of his reign and remains an important subject of historical discussion.

## Sulh-i Kul

Akbar's political thought became associated with the principle commonly described as sulh-i kul, or universal peace.

The concept represented an approach to governance that sought political stability and coexistence across religious and social divisions.

Akbar also reduced or removed several taxes associated with religious distinctions, including the jizya for a period of his reign.

His policies did not create a modern concept of secular government, but they represented a significant development in the political culture of the Mughal court.

## Fatehpur Sikri

Akbar established Fatehpur Sikri as an imperial center during the later sixteenth century.

The city became an important expression of Mughal architecture and court culture.

Its buildings combined elements associated with different architectural traditions of the subcontinent with forms developed within the Mughal imperial environment.

The complex included palaces, administrative spaces, religious buildings, and ceremonial areas.

Although the imperial court later moved away from Fatehpur Sikri, the city remains one of the most important architectural legacies of Akbar's reign.

## Court Culture

Akbar's court became a major center of artistic and intellectual activity.

Painting workshops produced illustrated manuscripts and developed a distinctive Mughal style that incorporated Persian traditions with artistic practices from the Indian subcontinent.

Histories commissioned at court, including the Akbarnama, presented an extensive account of the emperor and his reign.

The imperial court also supported literature, translation projects, architecture, music, and scholarship.

These activities helped create a cosmopolitan court culture that became influential throughout the Mughal world.

## Translation and Knowledge

Akbar's court sponsored translations of important works from Sanskrit and other Indian languages into Persian.

Among these projects was a Persian translation of the Mahabharata, known as the Razmnama.

Such projects were more than literary exercises.

They brought different intellectual traditions into contact within the imperial court and helped make Indian philosophical and literary material accessible to Persian-reading elites.

This translation culture became part of the wider intellectual environment of the Mughal Empire.

## Legacy

Akbar died in 1605 and was succeeded by his son Jahangir.

By the end of Akbar's reign, the Mughal Empire had become a major imperial power with extensive territory, substantial agricultural revenues, a powerful military structure, and an increasingly sophisticated administrative system.

His legacy was not simply the territory he conquered.

The political relationships, administrative practices, court culture, and intellectual traditions developed during his reign influenced the Mughal Empire for generations.

Akbar's reign therefore represents a major turning point in the history of early modern South Asia: an expanding military empire became a more deeply institutionalized imperial state.
