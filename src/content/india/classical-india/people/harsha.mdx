# Harsha

Harsha, or Harshavardhana, ruled much of northern India in the seventh century. He became one of the most prominent rulers of the period following the fragmentation of Gupta power.

## Why they matter

Harsha's reign provides a bridge between the Gupta age and the political landscape of early medieval north India. His court supported Sanskrit literary culture, religious institutions, diplomacy, and large public religious gatherings.

## Historical evidence

The principal sources include Bana's *Harshacharita*, Harsha's copper-plate inscriptions, other inscriptions such as the Banskhera and Madhuban records, coins, and the account of the Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang.

These sources do not agree perfectly. Xuanzang emphasizes Harsha's Buddhist patronage, while Harsha's own records and Bana's work also show strong Shaiva and Brahmanical elements.

## Traditions and later accounts

Later portrayals sometimes describe Harsha as a uniformly Buddhist king. The evidence suggests a more plural religious policy, with patronage extending across traditions.

Bana's *Harshacharita* is a courtly biography rather than a neutral chronicle, so its dramatic episodes require corroboration.
